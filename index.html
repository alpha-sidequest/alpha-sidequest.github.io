Space Defence

Space domain operations, capabilities, threats, and doctrine for the Australian Defence Force. Essential knowledge for Space Operations Officers, aircrew, and joint planners operating in a contested space environment.

ADF Space Defence Capabilities

The ADF is rapidly developing sovereign space capabilities to support joint operations, enable precision effects, and maintain space domain awareness. Key initiatives include protected satellite communications (JP9102 and follow-on programs), space domain awareness sensors (radars and telescopes), and small satellite / cubesat programs for resilient ISR and communications. Australia participates in US-led space architectures while pursuing sovereign options to reduce single points of failure.

  • Space Domain Awareness (SDA): Tracking satellites, debris, and threats in orbit. Critical for attribution and protection of ADF assets.
  • Protected Satcom: Anti-jam, secure communications resistant to adversary counterspace attacks.
  • Resilient Architectures: Proliferated LEO constellations, smallsats, and alternative PNT to survive attacks on GEO assets.
  • Ground Infrastructure: Distributed ground stations and command nodes less vulnerable to attack.

Adversary Space Capabilities (China & Russia)

Both China and Russia have mature counterspace programs designed to deny the US and its allies the use of space in a conflict.

China
Direct-ascent ASAT (SC-19, DN-3), co-orbital attack satellites, ground-based lasers and jammers, cyber attacks on satellite ground segments, and rapid launch reconstitution. China has demonstrated the ability to destroy LEO satellites and is developing capabilities against higher orbits.
Russia
Co-orbital ASAT systems (Nudol/PL-19), legacy direct-ascent, electronic warfare (jamming GPS and satcom), and cyber. Russia has conducted ASAT tests that created dangerous debris fields affecting all space users.

Allied Space Capabilities & Partnerships

Australia operates as part of a broader allied space enterprise, primarily with the United States.

  • United States: US Space Force provides GPS (with M-Code), SBIRS missile warning, AEHF protected comms, and proliferated LEO via Starshield. Australia has access arrangements and contributes sensors and ground infrastructure.
  • Five Eyes / AUKUS: Deep integration in space domain awareness, intelligence, and resilient comms. AUKUS Pillar II includes advanced space capabilities.
  • Commercial: Access to commercial mega-constellations (Starlink/Starshield derivatives) for surge capacity and resilience in denied environments.

Key Concepts Every Space Operations Officer Must Master

Orbital Regimes
LEO (low latency, high drag/debris), MEO (GPS), GEO (persistent comms). Different threats, physics, and response options apply to each.

Low Earth Orbit (LEO, ~160-2000 km): Used for high-resolution ISR, broadband comms (Starlink), and some navigation. Advantages: low latency, high resolution, cheaper to launch. Disadvantages: short dwell time over targets (need constellations), atmospheric drag requires frequent station-keeping, high debris density (Kessler risk highest here). Direct-ascent ASATs are most effective against LEO.

Medium Earth Orbit (MEO, ~2000-35,000 km): Primarily navigation (GPS, Galileo, BeiDou, GLONASS). Good global coverage with fewer satellites, moderate latency. Less debris than LEO but still vulnerable to certain ASATs. Critical for precision weapons and timing.

Geostationary Orbit (GEO, ~35,786 km): Fixed position over equator for persistent comms, weather, and missile warning (SBIRS). High latency (~0.5s round trip), expensive to launch, limited slots. Harder to attack kinetically but vulnerable to jamming, laser dazzling, and co-orbital threats. Ideal for strategic C2 but single points of failure if not proliferated.

Highly Elliptical Orbits (HEO) and others: Molniya for high-latitude coverage, etc. Understanding regime physics is essential for predicting satellite behavior, vulnerability windows, and maneuver options.
Counterspace Threats
Kinetic ASATs, co-orbital “inspector/killer” satellites, directed energy (lasers), EW/jamming of links, cyber attacks on ground segments and satellites, and supply-chain attacks on launch and manufacturing.

Kinetic Physical (Direct Ascent & Co-orbital): Missiles or satellites that physically destroy or disable targets. China’s SC-19/DN-3 and Russia’s Nudol have demonstrated LEO kills. Creates debris. Reversible? No. Detection via SSA.

Directed Energy (Lasers, RF): Ground or space-based lasers can blind or damage sensors (dazzling) or destroy electronics. Hard to attribute. Reversible if low power.

Electronic Warfare (Jamming/Spoofing): Uplink/downlink jamming of comms, PNT spoofing (fake GPS signals). Widely used (e.g. Russia in Ukraine, China in exercises). Reversible, hard to geo-locate sometimes. Mitigated by M-Code, directional antennas, inertial backup.

Cyber: Hacking ground stations, command links, or onboard systems. Can take control or cause denial. Supply chain (e.g. compromised chips in sats). Reversible with patches but attribution difficult.

Supply Chain & Launch Denial: Attacks on manufacturers, launch sites, or pre-positioned threats. Long-term effect on reconstitution.
Resilience & Reconstitution
Proliferated architectures, smallsats/cubesats, responsive launch, pre-positioned spares, and distributed ground infrastructure. “Shoot the archer, not the arrow.”

Proliferation & Disaggregation: Hundreds/thousands of small sats instead of a few large ones. Harder to kill all (e.g. Starshield). Redundancy in coverage and function.

Maneuver, Deception, Stealth: Onboard propulsion for evasion, decoys, low-observable designs, frequency hopping, burst transmissions.

Reconstitution: Pre-positioned spares on orbit or rapid launch capability (days/weeks not months/years). Commercial surge (SpaceX, etc.) as backup. Dispersed, hardened ground segments (mobile, underground, allied hosted).

“Shoot the Archer”: Prioritize attacking the adversary’s ASAT launchers, ground lasers, jammers, and C2 nodes rather than (or in addition to) the satellites themselves. This is a core tenet of space superiority planning – deny the counterspace capability at source.

An officer must plan for “space denied” operations from day one: what happens to ops when PNT, comms, and ISR are degraded or lost?
Space Law, Norms & Escalation
Outer Space Treaty (no WMD or claims of sovereignty), IHL in space, attribution challenges, and the risk that debris-generating attacks create long-term denial for everyone.

Outer Space Treaty 1967: Article I: exploration and use for benefit of all. Article II: no national appropriation. Article III: international law applies. Article IV: no WMD in orbit or on celestial bodies. No explicit ban on conventional ASATs or jamming.

International Humanitarian Law (IHL) in Space: Distinction (military vs civilian objects – hard when dual-use), proportionality (collateral damage to debris/global access), precautions. Attacks causing long-term debris may violate if excessive civilian harm (global comms, weather, etc.).

Attribution & Escalation: Hard to attribute (spoofing, false flags). Debris from ASAT can affect neutral parties (all spacefaring nations). Escalation ladder: reversible (jamming) → irreversible kinetic. Risk of miscalculation high. Norms developing via UN, but China/Russia often oppose binding rules.

Why it matters: A Space Ops Officer advises on legal risk of actions, ROE in space, and how to operate within (or challenge) norms without triggering broader conflict. Must understand “due regard” obligations and potential for space to become a domain of armed conflict.
Space Weather, Debris & Sustainability
Solar flares, radiation belts, and Kessler Syndrome (cascading collisions). Natural and man-made hazards can be as dangerous as deliberate attack.

Space Weather: Solar flares, coronal mass ejections (CMEs), geomagnetic storms can disrupt comms, GPS, power grids on Earth, and damage satellite electronics/radiation. Real-time monitoring (e.g. via US or allied) is critical for ops planning. ADF must factor in “space weather windows” for sensitive activities.

Orbital Debris: Millions of pieces >1cm tracked by USSPACECOM and others. Collision risk high in LEO. Every launch adds risk. ASAT tests (China 2007, Russia 2021) created thousands of pieces with decades-long lifetimes.

Kessler Syndrome: Cascade where one collision generates debris that causes more, potentially closing orbits for centuries. Not sci-fi – models show tipping points. Sustainability is national security: if LEO becomes unusable, modern military and economy suffer.

International Efforts: IADC (Inter-Agency Space Debris Coordination), UN COPUOS guidelines, active debris removal concepts (e.g. nets, harpoons, lasers). ADF contribution via SSA data sharing helps global catalog and conjunction warnings.

Officer Implication: Must advocate for responsible ops, understand conjunction assessment (avoiding collisions), and plan around natural hazards as part of threat/risk matrix.
Joint & Multi-Domain Integration
Space enables P-8A targeting, Army manoeuvre, Navy navigation, and air mobility. Officers must understand effects across domains and advise commanders on risk and opportunity.

Space as Enabler: PNT for precision munitions and navigation (without GPS, JDAMs become dumb bombs, aircraft lose accuracy). Satcom for beyond-line-of-sight C2, drone control, data links. ISR from space for targeting, BDA, maritime surveillance. Missile warning (SBIRS) for defense.

Space as Target & Dependency: Adversary will attack space first in high-end conflict. Degraded space = degraded everything (air, land, sea, cyber). “Space denied” CONOPS are essential: inertial nav, HF radio fallback, terrestrial comms, celestial navigation, local ISR.

Multi-Domain Command & Control (MDC2): Space effects must be integrated with air, land, maritime, cyber, info ops. A Space Ops Officer sits at the JOC or equivalent, providing space picture, recommending effects (e.g. jamming adversary satcom to support air strike), assessing risk to own assets.

Allied Integration: Deep reliance on US Space Force for many capabilities. Must understand sharing agreements, combined planning, and how AUKUS/Quad space cooperation enhances resilience.

Why Critical for Officer: You are the translator between space physics/capabilities and joint warfighting outcomes. You advise on when to accept risk in space vs when to protect it at all costs. Space is not a separate domain in practice – it is the backbone of the joint force.

ADF Leadership Doctrine

The ADF Leadership Model

Effective ADF leaders combine three interdependent elements:

Character
Who you are when no one is watching. Built on moral courage, integrity, and living the Defence values.
Professional Competence
Mastery of your trade, understanding of the wider context, and the ability to apply knowledge and skills under pressure.
Human Understanding
Self-awareness and insight into what motivates and affects others.

The Five Defence Values

These values underpin everything the ADF does. Leaders are expected to model them.

Service
Selflessness — placing the security and interests of the nation and its people ahead of your own.
Courage
The strength to do and say what is right, especially when difficult (physical and moral).
Respect
Valuing others and treating them with dignity. Requires humility and fairness.
Integrity
Consistency between what you think, say, and do. Being honest with yourself and others.
Excellence
Striving to be the best you can be — professionally and personally — every day.

The 10 ADF Leadership Principles

A practical framework for self-assessment and development at every level.

1. Know yourself and seek self-improvement
2. Be proficient — Master your trade and understand the broader context
3. Seek and accept responsibility
4. Lead by example

National Defence

The 2026 National Defence Strategy and 2026 Integrated Investment Program — Australia’s authoritative strategic and resourcing framework for an integrated, focused force.

Australia faces its most challenging strategic environment since the Second World War. Major power competition in the Indo-Pacific has intensified and broadened. The 2026 National Defence Strategy (NDS), released 16 April 2026 alongside the 2026 Integrated Investment Program (IIP), updates and strengthens the framework first set in the 2024 NDS.

Clarifying the Documents

People often confuse the independent review with the Government’s strategy and its funding plan. Here is the accurate sequence:

2023 Defence Strategic Review (DSR)
An independent review led by the Hon Stephen Smith and Air Chief Marshal Sir Angus Houston (Ret’d), commissioned by the Albanese Government and reported February 2023 (public version released April 2023). Conducted in just 28 weeks with a sense of urgency. It was not Government policy but a first-principles assessment recommending fundamental reform. The Review diagnosed that Australia’s strategic circumstances had deteriorated sharply: loss of warning time for major conflict (no longer the traditional 10 years), intense US-China competition as the defining feature of the Indo-Pacific, the return of major power strategic risk for the first time in 80 years, and the erosion of Australia’s geographic advantages due to long-range strike, cyber, and missile proliferation. It called for a shift from a “balanced force” (suited to low-level threats) to a “focused force” optimised for the Indo-Pacific, adoption of a Strategy of Denial, and a new conceptual approach of National Defence — a whole-of-government and whole-of-nation effort far broader than the old “Defence of Australia” doctrine.
2024 National Defence Strategy + IIP
Government’s first formal National Defence Strategy (April 2024). Translated the DSR recommendations into official policy. Introduced the concepts of National Defence (whole-of-nation) and Strategy of Denial. Established the biennial cycle. Accompanied by the first Integrated Investment Program.
2026 National Defence Strategy + IIP (Current)
Released 16 April 2026 by Deputy Prime Minister and Minister for Defence. Not a departure — a strengthening of resolve with greater emphasis on self-reliance, lessons from Ukraine and the Middle East, sovereign industrial capacity, national resilience/civil preparedness, and deeper regional partnerships. The accompanying 2026 IIP is the detailed resourcing roadmap.

Core Concepts

National Defence
A coordinated, whole-of-government and whole-of-nation approach that harnesses all arms of national power — diplomacy, industry, science & technology, intelligence, economic security, supply-chain resilience, civil preparedness and the military — to protect Australia’s security and advance its interests. It is broader than the old “Defence of Australia” concept.
Strategy of Denial
The cornerstone of defence planning. The aim is to deter a potential adversary from projecting military force against Australia or coercing us through the threat of force, by demonstrating the capability, capacity and resolve to make the costs and risks of aggression unacceptably high — before conflict begins. It spans all five domains and requires impactful projection, holding adversary forces at risk, and the ability to sustain operations.

The 2023 Defence Strategic Review — Key Findings & Recommendations

The Review provided a strategic assessment and a roadmap. Its public version is less detailed than the classified report, but it sets out the urgent case for reform. The Government largely accepted the direction and translated key elements into the 2024 National Defence Strategy and subsequent updates.

National Defence — Whole-of-Nation Approach

  • Replaces the “Defence of Australia” doctrine (focused on deterring low-level threats from small/middle powers in the immediate region).
  • Requires genuine whole-of-government and whole-of-nation effort: diplomacy/statecraft (DFAT leading), industry, science & technology, economic security, supply-chain resilience, civil preparedness, plus military power.
  • Active statecraft to support regional balance of power and deter coercion.

Strategic Circumstances & Warning Time

  • Loss of the traditional 10-year warning time for conventional conflict. Geography no longer provides reliable protection due to long-range precision strike, cyber, and power projection by regional actors (especially China’s unprecedented military build-up without transparency).
  • Three planning timeframes: urgent (2023–2025), medium (2026–2030), and beyond (2031+).
  • Highest strategic risk: prospect of major conflict in the region directly threatening Australia’s national interest.

The Integrated Force — Critical Capabilities

  • Undersea warfare (crewed & uncrewed) for persistent long-range ISR and strike.
  • Enhanced integrated targeting capability.
  • Enhanced long-range strike in all domains.
  • Fully enabled, integrated amphibious-capable combined-arms land system.
  • Enhanced all-domain maritime capabilities for sea denial and localised sea control.
  • Networked expeditionary air operations.
  • Enhanced all-domain integrated air and missile defence (IAMD).
  • Joint expeditionary theatre logistics with strategic depth.
  • Theatre command & control enabling the Integrated Force.
  • Developed network of northern bases for logistics, denial and deterrence.

Force Design Principles

  • Shift from “balanced force” to “focused force” optimised for the Indo-Pacific and Strategy of Denial.
  • Evolve from joint to genuinely Integrated Force across all five domains (maritime, land, air, space, cyber).
  • Strong support for conventionally-armed nuclear-powered submarines (AUKUS Pillar I) in the shortest possible timeframe and commitment to continuous naval shipbuilding.
  • Defence Science & Technology Group and new Advanced Strategic Capabilities Accelerator (ASCA) to focus on asymmetric advantage and critical technologies (AUKUS Pillar II).

Accelerated Preparedness, Acquisition Reform & Other Priorities

Posture & Northern Basing
Immediate upgrades and development of northern network of bases, ports and barracks. Fuel storage, supply and resilience must be rectified urgently. Enterprise-wide audit of estate and infrastructure. Leverage state/local and industry infrastructure in northern/central Australia.
Workforce
Significant challenges — both ADF and APS understrength while contractor workforce is the largest component. Bold changes to recruitment (new markets, policy/risk settings), retention (competitive pay/conditions/culture), centralised personnel management, and a comprehensive review of the ADF Reserves (including possible Ready Reserve Scheme).
Acquisition & Risk Reform
Current system “not fit for purpose.” Abandon pursuit of perfect solutions; focus on minimum viable capability in the shortest possible time. Reprioritise the Integrated Investment Program (IIP) — delay or cancel lower-priority projects to free resources and workforce for higher-priority capabilities. Risk-based behaviours (strategic risk over project-management risk). Value for money and timely delivery are the priority.
Finances & Biennial Strategy
More funding required; Defence spending must reflect the strategic circumstances. Move from intermittent White Papers to a biennial National Defence Strategy (first one no later than Q2 2024 — delivered as the 2024 NDS) for consistency and to keep pace with the environment.
Government Response (from the Review document): The Albanese Government agreed (or agreed in-principle) to the vast majority of the Review’s recommendations. It adopted six immediate priorities that directly flowed from the DSR: AUKUS nuclear-powered submarines; long-range strike and sovereign munitions; northern basing and posture; workforce growth and retention; rapid adoption of disruptive technologies (leading to ASCA); and deepening regional partnerships. Many detailed recommendations (e.g. specific project accelerations/cancellations, surface fleet analysis, GWEO enterprise leadership, fuel council) were to be further developed in the inaugural National Defence Strategy.

2026 National Defence Strategy — Key Elements

The 2026 NDS builds on the 2024 foundations in five explicit ways:

  1. Greater self-reliance over the longer term so Defence can more ably and independently deter force projection against Australia.
  2. Prioritise capability acquisition and sustainment drawing on lessons from the war in Ukraine and conflicts in the Middle East (attrition, mass of cheap systems, rapid innovation, industrial scaling, fuel & logistics resilience).
  3. Strengthen the resilience of Australia’s sovereign defence industrial base and build stronger, more diverse international industrial partnerships and supply chains.
  4. Improve national civil preparedness and national resilience as part of National Defence to help shield Australia from regional or global disruption.
  5. Increase coordination with regional partners to collectively deter actions inimical to shared interests and shape a region governed by rights and rules, not fear or force.
Self-reliance defined (official): “Increased self-reliance means enhancing Australia’s ability to employ and sustain credible military power to defend Australia in a crisis or conflict, including when support from allies or partners may be limited. Self-reliance does not mean self-sufficiency… It balances critical tasks Australia must be able to undertake ourselves with those that can be assured through trusted partnerships.”

The ADF’s Five Tasks (Strategy of Denial in action)

1. Defend Australia and our immediate region

The most consequential task: defend sovereign territory, people and economic connections against military attack by any potential adversary.

2. Deter through denial any potential adversary’s attempt to project power against Australia through our northern approaches

Demonstrate capability, capacity and resolve; work with allies/partners on collective deterrence.

3. Protect Australia’s economic connection to our region and the world

Safeguard sea lines of communication, maritime approaches and respond to coercion or threats to trade and supply chains.

4. Contribute with our partners to the collective security of the Indo-Pacific

Enhance maritime domain awareness, training, exercises, HADR and regional crisis response.

5. Contribute with our partners to protecting and upholding global rules and norms

Exercise rights under international law and support global peace and security in concert with allies.

Six Capability Effects the Integrated, Focused Force Must Deliver

1. Project force
Optimised for impactful projection to deter attempts to project power against Australia; rapidly deploy and employ forces.
2. Hold a potential adversary’s forces at risk
Credible ability to hold at risk forces that could target Australia’s interests, complicating their cost/risk calculus.
3. Protect ADF forces and supporting critical infrastructure in Australia
Protect personnel, facilities and information; withstand and recover from attack — signals resolve.
4. Sustain protracted combat operations
Ability to sustain operations despite disruptions to C2, infrastructure, logistics and communications.
5. Maintain persistent situational awareness in our primary area of military interest
Timely ISR across the immediate region and northern approaches; warning time and decision space for Government.
6. Achieve decision advantage
Exercise effective command and control in conflict, including under disruption, while undermining an adversary’s ability to do the same.

Planning uses three rolling epochs: Enhanced Force-in-Being (mid-2026 to mid-2028 — immediate enhancements to current force), Objective Integrated Force (mid-2028 to mid-2030 — accelerated acquisition of critical capabilities), and Future Integrated Force (mid-2030 and beyond — ADF fit for purpose across all five domains and enablers).

2026 Integrated Investment Program — The Funding Roadmap

The IIP translates strategy into concrete capability investment. The Government is allocating around $425 billion over the decade (2026-27 to 2035-36) to deliver the integrated, focused force. This includes an additional $14 billion over the next four years and $53 billion over the decade (new funding on top of the 2024 trajectory). Since coming to office the Albanese Government has increased defence spending by $30 billion over the forward estimates and $117 billion over the decade. Defence spending is currently 2.8% of GDP (highest among comparable like-minded Indo-Pacific countries) and is on track for 3% of GDP by 2033 under the NATO definition.

Current Cyber Threat Landscape

Australia faces one of the most persistent and sophisticated state-sponsored cyber threat environments in the world. State actors (overwhelmingly linked to China) are responsible for the majority of serious incidents affecting government and critical infrastructure. At the same time, profit-driven cyber criminals — primarily through ransomware and data extortion — represent the most frequent disruptive threat to Australian businesses and the broader economy (138 ransomware incidents responded to by ACSC in FY2024–25). The following sections detail both state espionage actors and the criminal/hybrid networks that also pose significant harm.

Chinese state-sponsored actors (primarily Ministry of State Security) represent the most significant and enduring cyber threat to Australia. Their operations are patient, highly resourced, and aligned with long-term strategic goals.

Key Chinese Groups Targeting Australia

APT40 (Leviathan / Kryptonite Panda)

One of the most active groups against Australian networks. Linked to the Hainan State Security Department (Ministry of State Security).

  • Focus: Maritime, defence industry, engineering, aerospace, and government entities.
  • Known Successes Against Australia: Multiple compromises of Australian defence contractors and maritime research organisations (2018–2024). Intellectual property theft from shipbuilding and underwater systems programs.
  • Vulnerabilities Exploited: Rapid exploitation of public-facing vulnerabilities (e.g., Microsoft Exchange, VPN appliances), supply-chain compromise via third-party vendors, and living-off-the-land techniques.
  • Damages: Theft of sensitive defence technology and research data over multi-year periods. Persistent access in some networks for 12+ months.
Threat Assessment: Critical
Highest volume and most consistent threat to Australian defence and maritime sectors. Demonstrated ability to rapidly weaponise new vulnerabilities and maintain long-term persistence.

Salt Typhoon (Telecom Espionage)

Specialises in compromising telecommunications providers to gain persistent access to backbone and edge networks.

  • Known Successes: 2024–2025 activity observed targeting Australian telecommunications infrastructure. Similar campaigns successfully compromised major US and European telcos.
  • Vulnerabilities Exploited: Router and edge device vulnerabilities, weak segmentation between management and customer networks, and supply-chain attacks on networking equipment.
  • Damages: Potential access to call records, subscriber data, and the ability to enable future communications interception at scale.
Threat Assessment: High
Direct risk to Australia’s critical communications infrastructure. Pre-positioning in telcos enables both espionage and potential wartime disruption.

Volt Typhoon (Pre-positioning)

Assessed as preparing for potential disruptive or destructive attacks in a future conflict scenario.

  • Known Successes Against Australia: 2023–2025 ASD assessments confirmed Chinese state actors had gained access to multiple Australian critical infrastructure networks (energy, water, transport, and ports) using Volt Typhoon tactics.
  • Vulnerabilities Exploited: Living-off-the-land (PowerShell, WMI, native admin tools), unpatched internet-facing devices, weak network segmentation, and supply-chain compromise of operational technology (OT) vendors.
  • Damages: Long-term persistence (in some cases years) with the ability to map networks, identify control systems, and potentially cause disruption to essential services during a crisis. No public destructive attacks yet, but clear pre-positioning.
Threat Assessment: Critical
Highest strategic concern. Not about theft — this is preparation for wartime effects against Australian society and military mobilisation.

APT41 (Double Dragon)

MSS-linked group with both state espionage and financially motivated operations.

  • Known Successes: Supply-chain attacks and telecommunications targeting against Australian organisations (including healthcare and government contractors).
  • Vulnerabilities Exploited: Software supply chain (SolarWinds-style tactics observed globally), zero-day exploitation, and credential harvesting.
  • Damages: Theft of sensitive data and deployment of ransomware in some cases for financial gain alongside espionage.
Threat Assessment: High
Dual mission makes attribution and response more complex. High volume of activity against Australian targets.

Russia

Sandworm (Voodoo Bear / APT44) – GRU

Russian military intelligence unit responsible for destructive attacks globally (NotPetya, Ukraine power grid attacks).

  • Known Activity Against Australia: Increased espionage and disruptive probing of Australian government and defence networks since 2022 (post-Ukraine invasion). Some successful initial access via spear-phishing and supply-chain attacks on third-party vendors.
  • Vulnerabilities Exploited: Phishing, exploitation of public vulnerabilities in internet-facing services, and living-off-the-land.
  • Damages: Theft of sensitive government and defence information. Limited public disruptive effects in Australia to date.
Threat Assessment: High
Capable of high-impact disruptive attacks (demonstrated globally). Currently more focused on espionage against Australia but retains destructive capability.

Cozy Bear (APT29) – SVR

Russian foreign intelligence service focused on long-term espionage.

  • Known Activity: Targeting of Australian government agencies, universities, and think tanks. Some successful access to sensitive research and policy information.
  • Vulnerabilities: Spear-phishing, exploitation of cloud misconfigurations, and supply-chain attacks.
Threat Assessment: Medium-High
Patient, high-skill espionage actor. Less likely to cause immediate disruption than Sandworm but excellent at long-term intelligence collection.

North Korea

Lazarus Group (APT38 / Hidden Cobra)

North Korea’s most capable and aggressive cyber unit. Responsible for major bank heists and crypto theft worldwide.

  • Known Activity Against Australia: Multiple attempts and some successes against Australian crypto exchanges and financial entities for funding. Espionage targeting of defence research and technology companies.
  • Vulnerabilities Exploited: Sophisticated social engineering, supply-chain attacks on software vendors, and exploitation of cryptocurrency platforms.
  • Damages: Financial losses in the millions (globally) and theft of sensitive defence-related intellectual property.
Threat Assessment: Medium-High
Highly motivated by financial gain to fund the regime. Increasing capability in espionage. Less focused on Australia than China but still a credible threat to financial and research sectors.

Iran

APT33 (Elfin) and Charming Kitten

Iranian state-sponsored groups focused on espionage and disruptive attacks against Western and regional adversaries.

  • Known Activity Against Australia: Limited high-profile successes publicly attributed. Primarily opportunistic espionage against government, energy, and research targets. Some hacktivist-style defacements and data leaks during periods of tension.
  • Vulnerabilities Exploited: Spear-phishing, exploitation of known vulnerabilities in web applications, and credential stuffing.
  • Damages: Theft of sensitive information and minor disruptive effects. Lower capability and focus on Australia compared to China or Russia.
Threat Assessment: Medium
Capable but currently lower priority threat to Australia. More focused on the Middle East and the United States. Could increase activity during periods of geopolitical tension.
Why this matters for a Cyber Warfare Officer:
These are not random criminals — they are state intelligence services running long-term campaigns against Australia’s defence industry and critical systems. A good CWO understands both the technical tradecraft and the strategic intent behind these operations, and knows which threats require immediate effects vs long-term intelligence preparation.

Non-State Cyber Criminal Networks & State-Affiliated Criminal Actors

While state-sponsored espionage (detailed above) poses the most serious long-term risk to government, defence and critical infrastructure, profit-driven cyber criminals are the most frequent and disruptive threat to Australian businesses, the economy, and individuals. In FY2024–25, ASD’s ACSC responded to 138 ransomware incidents. Ransomware represented 11% of incidents but a far higher proportion of high-impact cases. The majority of serious cybercrime is financially motivated and originates from Russian-speaking or Eastern European ecosystems, often operating as Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS) with global affiliates.

Key Non-State Cyber Criminal Groups

BianLian (Russia-based RaaS / Data Extortion)

A ransomware developer, deployer and data extortion cybercriminal group, assessed as likely Russia-based with multiple Russia-based affiliates. Jointly tracked by ACSC, FBI and CISA.

  • Australian Impact: Predominantly targeted private enterprises, including at least one critical infrastructure organisation, professional services and property development sectors. Part of the broader criminal wave causing rising data breaches and extortion demands.
  • Tactics & Capability: Initial access frequently via purchased or stolen valid Remote Desktop Protocol (RDP) credentials. Uses open-source tools and command-line scripting for discovery and credential harvesting. Exfiltrates data via FTP, Rclone or Mega. Originally used double-extortion (exfil + encrypt); shifted around 2023–2024 to primarily or exclusively exfiltration-based extortion — steal data and threaten to publish it on the dark web regardless of payment.
  • Intent: Pure financial gain through multi-layered extortion. High volume, professionalised operations typical of the Russian cybercrime ecosystem. Benefits from bulletproof hosting and money-laundering infrastructure.
Threat Assessment: High (economy & selected CI)
Updated joint ACSC/FBI/CISA advisory (Nov 2024) details evolving TTPs. Contributes directly to the surge in reported data breaches and financial losses for Australian organisations. Enablers of this ecosystem have been subject to coordinated international sanctions.

LockBit & Major RaaS Affiliates (Clop, BlackSuit/Blacksuit & successors)

LockBit has been one of the most active and damaging Ransomware-as-a-Service operations globally and in Australia (historically accounting for a significant share — e.g. ~18% in some reporting periods — of AU ransomware incidents). Core operators supply the malware and leak infrastructure; affiliates (including some linked to other groups) execute the attacks.

  • Australian & Allied Harm: Multiple confirmed incidents against Australian businesses (including financial/reinsurance and other sectors). Globally, LockBit and similar groups have hit thousands of victims across healthcare, government, manufacturing and critical services in Five Eyes nations and allies. Australian Federal Police (AFP) has participated in major international disruption operations against LockBit infrastructure and affiliates.
  • Capability: Mature affiliate model, rapid exploitation of public vulnerabilities, sophisticated data exfiltration before or instead of encryption, professional leak sites for extortion pressure, and use of initial access brokers. Supported by dark web services for hosting, bulletproof infrastructure and crypto laundering.
  • Other Notable Groups: Clop (specialises in large-scale data extortion, often via supply-chain compromises such as MOVEit); BlackSuit (successor-style to Royal/Conti, aggressive targeting of healthcare and manufacturing with double extortion).
Threat Assessment: High (volume & economic damage)
RaaS model lowers the barrier for less-skilled actors while maintaining high capability. International law enforcement actions (US, UK, Australia, Europol etc.) have disrupted infrastructure but the model is resilient and quickly re-forms under new brands or leadership.

State-Affiliated Cyber Criminal Networks (Hybrid Threats)

Lazarus Group (DPRK State-Sponsored – APT38 / Hidden Cobra)

North Korea’s most capable and aggressive cyber unit, directed by the state (under structures such as Lab 110 / Bureau 121). While it conducts classic espionage, it is uniquely and heavily engaged in large-scale financial crime — bank heists, cryptocurrency exchange thefts and ransomware — to generate hard currency for the regime’s nuclear weapons and ballistic missile programmes.

  • Scale of Criminal Operations: Responsible for theft of hundreds of millions to over $1.5 billion in individual incidents (e.g. Bybit cold wallet heist and multiple prior exchange/bank compromises). Cumulative hauls estimated in the billions. Uses advanced social engineering, supply-chain compromise, and crypto-specific laundering techniques.
  • Australia-Specific Activity: Multiple attempts and some successes against Australian crypto exchanges and financial entities (for funding). Concurrent espionage targeting of defence research and technology companies. Australia has joined international sanctions against DPRK-linked cyber actors.
  • Threat to Australia and Allies: Direct economic harm to the financial and crypto sectors. Funds an adversarial state’s strategic capabilities. Extremely high technical capability that overlaps with pure state espionage tradecraft. Global operations affect Five Eyes financial systems, critical infrastructure (via theft or potential disruptive follow-on), and the broader rules-based order. Classic example of a state using criminal methods for deniability and revenue.
Threat Assessment: High (Hybrid State-Criminal)
Not a pure criminal group — it is state-directed cybercrime at industrial scale. Poses both immediate financial and longer-term strategic risks. Overlaps significantly with the espionage threats detailed earlier in this section.

Evil Corp & Russian Cybercrime Enablers (State Ties / Protection)

A long-running Russia-based cybercriminal organisation (active since at least 2009 with Dridex banking malware). Prolific in credential theft, financial fraud and ransomware (both direct operations and through affiliates, including documented links to LockBit actors). One of the most significant cybercrime threats historically, with impacts across 40+ countries and over $100 million in documented losses in earlier campaigns alone.

  • Australian Impact: Malware campaigns specifically targeting Australians and Australian financial institutions. Provided infrastructure and services that enabled ransomware attacks (including by syndicates such as LockBit, BlackSuit and Clop) against Australian and international businesses, resulting in data exfiltration, encryption and extortion.
  • State Affiliation: Extensive and documented relationship with Russian intelligence services. The group and its enablers have operated with a significant degree of impunity inside Russia. Coordinated trilateral sanctions actions (Australia + United States + United Kingdom, 2024–2025) targeted senior members (e.g. Maksim Viktorovich Yakubets and others) and key service providers such as bulletproof hosting companies (Media Land LLC, ML Cloud LLC) that supported the broader criminal ecosystem.
  • Capability & Intent: Highly professionalised: custom malware development, affiliate recruitment, bulletproof hosting, money laundering. The infrastructure they and similar groups provide lowers barriers for many “non-state” actors. The state ties create safe haven and potential for tasking or intelligence sharing when it suits Russian interests.
Threat Assessment: High (with state protection/overlap)
Not purely non-state. The blending of criminal profit motives with state protection and occasional alignment makes attribution and disruption more complex. Directly harms Australian businesses and supports the wider ransomware economy that affects allies.
Why this matters for a Cyber Warfare Officer:
The line between “state” and “criminal” is often blurry and operationally relevant. Criminal groups (especially Russian-speaking RaaS and IABs) frequently provide the initial access that state actors later exploit or purchase. Lazarus is the textbook case of a state running an industrial-scale criminal enterprise to fund its strategic programmes. Evil Corp-style groups enjoy Russian state protection while harming Western economies. A strong CWO must:
  • Understand the criminal ecosystem’s TTPs (many identical to state tradecraft: infostealers, RDP compromise, living-off-the-land, supply-chain abuse).
  • Recognise how paying ransoms or failing to disrupt enablers (hosting, laundering) directly funds state adversaries (especially North Korea).
  • Appreciate the role of sanctions, law enforcement disruption (AFP + ASD’s Operation Aquila and international ops), and public-private partnerships in raising costs for these actors.
  • Defend the economy and critical infrastructure against ransomware as a core national security mission — not just a “crime” problem.
  • Be prepared for hybrid scenarios where criminal access becomes a vector for state pre-positioning or destructive effects.

Sources: ASD ACSC Annual Cyber Threat Report 2024–25; joint ACSC/FBI/CISA BianLian advisory (updated Nov 2024); Australian, US and UK sanctions announcements on Evil Corp and enablers (2024–2025); international law enforcement disruption reporting (LockBit operations involving AFP).

Known Successful Preventions & Mitigations

While these actors are highly capable, several defensive measures have repeatedly proven effective at detecting, evicting, or preventing their operations against Australian networks.

Against Chinese Pre-Positioning (Volt Typhoon-Style)

Network Segmentation & Zero Trust
Multiple Australian organisations detected and evicted Chinese actors after implementing strict segmentation between IT and OT networks. Lateral movement was blocked even after initial compromise of internet-facing systems.
Living-Off-The-Land Detection
ASD and partners have reported successful detection of Volt Typhoon activity through behavioural analytics looking for unusual use of PowerShell, WMI, and native admin tools — techniques these actors rely on to stay stealthy.
Rapid Patching of Edge Devices
Organisations that maintained aggressive patching cycles for VPNs, firewalls, and remote access gateways significantly reduced initial access opportunities. Many Chinese campaigns begin with known vulnerabilities in these devices.

Against APT40 & Supply-Chain Attacks

Essential Eight Maturity
Australian organisations at higher Essential Eight maturity levels have repeatedly detected and blocked APT40’s rapid exploitation attempts. Application control and patching have been particularly effective at stopping their “smash and grab” style operations.
Third-Party & Supply Chain Scrutiny
After several high-profile compromises through defence contractors and vendors, increased vendor security assessments and SBOM requirements have reduced successful supply-chain intrusions by groups like APT41.
Multi-Factor Authentication + Monitoring
Mandatory MFA combined with monitoring for unusual authentication patterns has stopped many credential-harvesting campaigns that APT40 relies on heavily.

Against Russian, North Korean & Iranian Actors

Email Security & User Awareness
Strong email filtering combined with regular phishing simulations has significantly reduced successful initial access by Russian GRU-linked groups and North Korean Lazarus operators, who still rely heavily on spear-phishing.
Cloud Configuration Hardening
Many Russian and Iranian espionage attempts target misconfigured cloud environments. Organisations that implemented strict conditional access, logging, and least-privilege principles have successfully detected and blocked these campaigns.
Crypto & Financial Sector Defences
Australian financial and crypto entities that adopted advanced transaction monitoring and multi-layered wallet security have successfully thwarted multiple North Korean Lazarus attempts to steal funds.
Key Lesson for Cyber Warfare Officers:
The most effective defences against these sophisticated state actors are often the same basic controls applied rigorously: patching, segmentation, application control, MFA, and behavioural detection. The difference between success and failure is usually speed of detection and the ability to limit an attacker’s freedom of movement once they are inside the network.
