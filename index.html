ADF Leadership Doctrine

The ADF Leadership Model

Effective ADF leaders combine three interdependent elements:

Character
Who you are when no one is watching. Built on moral courage, integrity, and living the Defence values.
Professional Competence
Mastery of your trade, understanding of the wider context, and the ability to apply knowledge and skills under pressure.
Human Understanding
Self-awareness and insight into what motivates and affects others.

The Five Defence Values

These values underpin everything the ADF does. Leaders are expected to model them.

Service
Selflessness — placing the security and interests of the nation and its people ahead of your own.
Courage
The strength to do and say what is right, especially when difficult (physical and moral).
Respect
Valuing others and treating them with dignity. Requires humility and fairness.
Integrity
Consistency between what you think, say, and do. Being honest with yourself and others.
Excellence
Striving to be the best you can be — professionally and personally — every day.

The 10 ADF Leadership Principles

A practical framework for self-assessment and development at every level.

1. Know yourself and seek self-improvement
2. Be proficient — Master your trade and understand the broader context
3. Seek and accept responsibility
4. Lead by example
5. Provide direction and keep your team informed
6. Know and care for your people
7. Develop the potential of your people
8. Make sound and timely decisions
9. Build the team and challenge its abilities
10. Communicate effectively

Mission Command

The ADF’s preferred command philosophy — centralised intent and decentralised execution. Leaders provide clear purpose and intent (the “what” and “why”); competent subordinates decide “how”.

Mutual trust
Built on shared values
Shared understanding
Common picture of the situation
Clear commander’s intent
The “what” and “why”
Mission-style orders
Focus on what/why, not excessive “how”
Disciplined initiative
Subordinates act within intent
Prudent risk management
Calculated, not reckless
Verification & feedback
Continuous alignment

Leadership Styles in Context

Effective leaders adapt their style while remaining anchored in Defence values:

Transformational
Inspires and motivates toward a shared vision (highly valued in the ADF).
Transactional
Clear tasks, rewards, and accountability — useful in high-tempo or training environments.
Servant Leadership
Prioritises growth and wellbeing of people (aligns with “know and care for your people”).
Authoritative / Directive
Necessary in crisis or when speed and clarity are critical.
Participative / Democratic
Useful when time allows and team buy-in is important.

Developing Leaders

The ADF believes leadership can and must be developed through:

Education
Formal courses and professional military education
Experience
Challenging appointments and operational service
Self-development
Reflection, reading, seeking feedback, deliberate practice

Good leaders invest in developing their people and preparing their successors.

National Defence

Australia’s evolving strategic framework — from the 2023 Defence Strategic Review to the current 2026 National Defence Strategy.

Australia faces its most challenging strategic environment since the Second World War. Major power competition in the Indo-Pacific has intensified, with reduced warning time for potential conflict. The 2023 Defence Strategic Review (DSR) was the catalyst for a fundamental rethink of Australia’s defence planning.

Core Concepts

National Defence
A whole-of-nation approach. Defence is no longer viewed as the responsibility of the military alone — it requires the integration of diplomacy, industry, science, technology, and national resilience.
Strategy of Denial
The central strategic idea: to deter an adversary from projecting military force into Australia’s region by making the costs and risks of aggression too high.

2023 Defence Strategic Review (DSR)

An independent review commissioned by the Albanese Government. Released April 2023 — it fundamentally changed how Australia thinks about defence.

Key Shifts Recommended

  • Move from a “balanced force” to a “focused force” optimised for the Indo-Pacific
  • Adopt Strategy of Denial as the foundation of defence planning
  • Significantly improve long-range strike, munitions stocks, and northern basing
  • Accelerate capability delivery and accept more risk in acquisition

Government’s Six Priority Areas

  1. AUKUS nuclear-powered submarines
  2. Long-range strike and sovereign munitions production
  3. Northern basing and posture
  4. Workforce growth and retention
  5. Rapid adoption of disruptive technologies (led to ASCA)
  6. Deepening regional partnerships

Evolution of the National Defence Strategy

2024 National Defence Strategy
Government’s first formal National Defence Strategy. Translated the 2023 DSR into official policy. Adopted National Defence and Strategy of Denial as the strategic framework. Confirmed the shift to a focused, integrated force. Marked the start of a biennial strategy cycle.
2026 National Defence Strategy (Current)
Describes an even more dangerous environment than 2024. Places significantly greater emphasis on self-reliance and sovereign industrial capacity. Incorporates lessons from Ukraine and the Middle East. Further accelerates priority capabilities and strengthens focus on national resilience and civil preparedness.

Current Cyber Threat Landscape

Australia faces one of the most persistent and sophisticated state-sponsored cyber threat environments in the world. State actors (overwhelmingly linked to China) are responsible for the majority of serious incidents affecting government and critical infrastructure.

Chinese state-sponsored actors (primarily Ministry of State Security) represent the most significant and enduring cyber threat to Australia. Their operations are patient, highly resourced, and aligned with long-term strategic goals.

Key Chinese Groups Targeting Australia

APT40 (Leviathan / Kryptonite Panda)

One of the most active groups against Australian networks. Linked to the Hainan State Security Department (Ministry of State Security).

  • Focus: Maritime, defence industry, engineering, aerospace, and government entities.
  • Known Successes Against Australia: Multiple compromises of Australian defence contractors and maritime research organisations (2018–2024). Intellectual property theft from shipbuilding and underwater systems programs.
  • Vulnerabilities Exploited: Rapid exploitation of public-facing vulnerabilities (e.g., Microsoft Exchange, VPN appliances), supply-chain compromise via third-party vendors, and living-off-the-land techniques.
  • Damages: Theft of sensitive defence technology and research data over multi-year periods. Persistent access in some networks for 12+ months.
Threat Assessment: Critical
Highest volume and most consistent threat to Australian defence and maritime sectors. Demonstrated ability to rapidly weaponise new vulnerabilities and maintain long-term persistence.

Salt Typhoon (Telecom Espionage)

Specialises in compromising telecommunications providers to gain persistent access to backbone and edge networks.

  • Known Successes: 2024–2025 activity observed targeting Australian telecommunications infrastructure. Similar campaigns successfully compromised major US and European telcos.
  • Vulnerabilities Exploited: Router and edge device vulnerabilities, weak segmentation between management and customer networks, and supply-chain attacks on networking equipment.
  • Damages: Potential access to call records, subscriber data, and the ability to enable future communications interception at scale.
Threat Assessment: High
Direct risk to Australia’s critical communications infrastructure. Pre-positioning in telcos enables both espionage and potential wartime disruption.

Volt Typhoon (Pre-positioning)

Assessed as preparing for potential disruptive or destructive attacks in a future conflict scenario.

  • Known Successes Against Australia: 2023–2025 ASD assessments confirmed Chinese state actors had gained access to multiple Australian critical infrastructure networks (energy, water, transport, and ports) using Volt Typhoon tactics.
  • Vulnerabilities Exploited: Living-off-the-land (PowerShell, WMI, native admin tools), unpatched internet-facing devices, weak network segmentation, and supply-chain compromise of operational technology (OT) vendors.
  • Damages: Long-term persistence (in some cases years) with the ability to map networks, identify control systems, and potentially cause disruption to essential services during a crisis. No public destructive attacks yet, but clear pre-positioning.
Threat Assessment: Critical
Highest strategic concern. Not about theft — this is preparation for wartime effects against Australian society and military mobilisation.

APT41 (Double Dragon)

MSS-linked group with both state espionage and financially motivated operations.

  • Known Successes: Supply-chain attacks and telecommunications targeting against Australian organisations (including healthcare and government contractors).
  • Vulnerabilities Exploited: Software supply chain (SolarWinds-style tactics observed globally), zero-day exploitation, and credential harvesting.
  • Damages: Theft of sensitive data and deployment of ransomware in some cases for financial gain alongside espionage.
Threat Assessment: High
Dual mission makes attribution and response more complex. High volume of activity against Australian targets.

Russia

Sandworm (Voodoo Bear / APT44) – GRU

Russian military intelligence unit responsible for destructive attacks globally (NotPetya, Ukraine power grid attacks).

  • Known Activity Against Australia: Increased espionage and disruptive probing of Australian government and defence networks since 2022 (post-Ukraine invasion). Some successful initial access via spear-phishing and supply-chain attacks on third-party vendors.
  • Vulnerabilities Exploited: Phishing, exploitation of public vulnerabilities in internet-facing services, and living-off-the-land.
  • Damages: Theft of sensitive government and defence information. Limited public disruptive effects in Australia to date.
Threat Assessment: High
Capable of high-impact disruptive attacks (demonstrated globally). Currently more focused on espionage against Australia but retains destructive capability.

Cozy Bear (APT29) – SVR

Russian foreign intelligence service focused on long-term espionage.

North Korea

Lazarus Group (APT38 / Hidden Cobra)

North Korea’s most capable and aggressive cyber unit. Responsible for major bank heists and crypto theft worldwide.

  • Known Activity Against Australia: Multiple attempts and some successes against Australian crypto exchanges and financial entities for funding. Espionage targeting of defence research and technology companies.
  • Vulnerabilities Exploited: Sophisticated social engineering, supply-chain attacks on software vendors, and exploitation of cryptocurrency platforms.
  • Damages: Financial losses in the millions (globally) and theft of sensitive defence-related intellectual property.
Threat Assessment: Medium-High
Highly motivated by financial gain to fund the regime. Increasing capability in espionage. Less focused on Australia than China but still a credible threat to financial and research sectors.

Iran

APT33 (Elfin) and Charming Kitten

Iranian state-sponsored groups focused on espionage and disruptive attacks against Western and regional adversaries.

  • Known Activity Against Australia: Limited high-profile successes publicly attributed. Primarily opportunistic espionage against government, energy, and research targets. Some hacktivist-style defacements and data leaks during periods of tension.
  • Vulnerabilities Exploited: Spear-phishing, exploitation of known vulnerabilities in web applications, and credential stuffing.
  • Damages: Theft of sensitive information and minor disruptive effects. Lower capability and focus on Australia compared to China or Russia.
Threat Assessment: Medium
Capable but currently lower priority threat to Australia. More focused on the Middle East and the United States. Could increase activity during periods of geopolitical tension.
Why this matters for a Cyber Warfare Officer:
These are not random criminals — they are state intelligence services running long-term campaigns against Australia’s defence industry and critical systems. A good CWO understands both the technical tradecraft and the strategic intent behind these operations, and knows which threats require immediate effects vs long-term intelligence preparation.

Known Successful Preventions & Mitigations

While these actors are highly capable, several defensive measures have repeatedly proven effective at detecting, evicting, or preventing their operations against Australian networks.

Against Chinese Pre-Positioning (Volt Typhoon-Style)

Network Segmentation & Zero Trust
Multiple Australian organisations detected and evicted Chinese actors after implementing strict segmentation between IT and OT networks. Lateral movement was blocked even after initial compromise of internet-facing systems.
Living-Off-The-Land Detection
ASD and partners have reported successful detection of Volt Typhoon activity through behavioural analytics looking for unusual use of PowerShell, WMI, and native admin tools — techniques these actors rely on to stay stealthy.
Rapid Patching of Edge Devices
Organisations that maintained aggressive patching cycles for VPNs, firewalls, and remote access gateways significantly reduced initial access opportunities. Many Chinese campaigns begin with known vulnerabilities in these devices.

Against APT40 & Supply-Chain Attacks

Essential Eight Maturity
Australian organisations at higher Essential Eight maturity levels have repeatedly detected and blocked APT40’s rapid exploitation attempts. Application control and patching have been particularly effective at stopping their “smash and grab” style operations.
Third-Party & Supply Chain Scrutiny
After several high-profile compromises through defence contractors and vendors, increased vendor security assessments and SBOM requirements have reduced successful supply-chain intrusions by groups like APT41.
Multi-Factor Authentication + Monitoring
Mandatory MFA combined with monitoring for unusual authentication patterns has stopped many credential-harvesting campaigns that APT40 relies on heavily.

Against Russian, North Korean & Iranian Actors

Email Security & User Awareness
Strong email filtering combined with regular phishing simulations has significantly reduced successful initial access by Russian GRU-linked groups and North Korean Lazarus operators, who still rely heavily on spear-phishing.
Cloud Configuration Hardening
Many Russian and Iranian espionage attempts target misconfigured cloud environments. Organisations that implemented strict conditional access, logging, and least-privilege principles have successfully detected and blocked these campaigns.
Crypto & Financial Sector Defences
Australian financial and crypto entities that adopted advanced transaction monitoring and multi-layered wallet security have successfully thwarted multiple North Korean Lazarus attempts to steal funds.
Key Lesson for Cyber Warfare Officers:
The most effective defences against these sophisticated state actors are often the same basic controls applied rigorously: patching, segmentation, application control, MFA, and behavioural detection. The difference between success and failure is usually speed of detection and the ability to limit an attacker’s freedom of movement once they are inside the network.

AI and Its Impact on Cybersecurity

Artificial Intelligence is fundamentally changing both offensive and defensive cyber operations.

⚔️ Offensive Use of AI (Adversaries)

  • Automated vulnerability discovery at massive scale
  • AI-generated polymorphic malware that evades detection
  • Deepfake-enabled social engineering & influence ops
  • Autonomous cyber weapons that adapt in real time

🛡️ Defensive Use of AI (ADF/ASD)

  • Real-time threat detection at machine speed
  • Automated incident response via SOAR platforms
  • AI-powered threat hunting across huge datasets
  • Adversarial AI defence & model hardening

The ADF and ASD are actively adopting AI for defence while preparing for an environment where adversaries use AI-enabled tools as standard.

Specific AI Use Cases in Cyber Operations

Offensive / Adversary Use Cases

  • Deepfake voice/video for targeted spear-phishing at scale

  • AI-powered threat hunting across massive log/graph datasets
  • Adversarial robustness testing of own AI systems (red-teaming ML models)
  • Automated malware reverse-engineering & attribution support
  • Generative AI for rapid scenario & tabletop exercise generation

Supply Chain Risks

One of the most significant and under-appreciated risks in modern cyber operations.

Hardware & FirmwareBackdoors or compromised firmware in networking gear, servers, and IoT used across Defence and critical infrastructure.
Software Supply ChainSolarWinds-style attacks where trusted vendors are compromised to deliver malicious updates.
Foreign Cloud & ChipsReliance on foreign providers creates chokepoints and legal exposure (e.g. US CLOUD Act).
Critical Minerals & SemiconductorsManufacturing concentration (China/Taiwan) creates strategic vulnerability for future AI/cyber capability.
Third-Party ContractorsManaged service providers often represent the weakest link in the defensive perimeter.

For CWO applicants: Demonstrating awareness of supply chain risk — and how sovereign initiatives mitigate it — is a strong indicator of strategic thinking.
